<Section id="origin">

In 1990 Schapire proved something surprising: a pile of rules that are each only slightly better than guessing can be combined into one that's as accurate as you like. AdaBoost (Freund and Schapire, 1995) did it by **re-weighting** the rows each new rule got wrong. Then in 1999–2001 Jerome Friedman, with Breiman's insight in the background, reframed the whole thing: boosting is **gradient descent**, where each step is a small tree. That framing let boosting use **any differentiable loss**: squared error, absolute error, quantiles, Poisson counts, log loss.

**What it fixed:** a single tree is unstable and blocky; a random forest averages trees but can't push past their shared bias. Boosting attacks **bias** directly: each tree is aimed at exactly what the model still gets wrong, measured by whichever loss the business cares about.

You met the mechanics in [Boosting by Hand](/learn/models/boosting-by-hand): mean, residuals, a stump, add half, repeat. This page generalizes that recipe to other losses, adds XGBoost's second-order step, and shows where boosting breaks.

<KeyIdea>

**Gradient boosting is gradient descent on the predictions.** Each round computes, for every row, which direction the loss wants that row's prediction to move (the negative gradient). A small tree learns that pattern, so the step generalizes to new rows. Change the loss and you change the arrows, nothing else.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section id="hand">

The same six stores as the Keystone: store size (thousand sq ft) and drills sold per week. Learning rate 0.5, stumps.

| Store | A | B | C | D | E | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Size | 20 | 30 | 40 | 55 | 70 | 90 |
| Drills | 10 | 18 | 14 | 34 | 40 | 52 |

<Steps>
<Step>

**Squared error (the Keystone).** Start at the mean, 28. The negative gradient of $\tfrac12(y - F)^2$ is the residual $y - F$: −18, −10, −14, 6, 12, 24. The stump splits at 47.5 with leaves −14 and +14, and the predictions become **21** and **35**.

</Step>
<Step>

**Absolute error.** The best constant for absolute error is the **median**: $(18 + 34)/2 = 26$. The negative gradient of $\lvert y - F\rvert$ is just the **sign** of the residual:

| Store | A | B | C | D | E | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Residual $y - 26$ | −16 | −8 | −12 | 8 | 14 | 26 |
| Pseudo-residual (sign) | −1 | −1 | −1 | +1 | +1 | +1 |

A stump on the signs splits at 47.5 again. But a leaf's value isn't the mean of the signs: it's re-estimated as the value that minimizes absolute error inside the leaf, the **median residual**. Left: median of −16, −8, −12 is **−12**. Right: median of 8, 14, 26 is **14**. With learning rate 0.5 the predictions become $26 - 6 =$ **20** and $26 + 7 =$ **33**.

Store F's big residual (26) counts as just +1. Absolute-error boosting doesn't care how wrong a row is, only which way.

</Step>
<Step>

**Poisson (counts, log link).** The model's raw score is $F = \log(\text{rate})$, starting at $\log 28$. The negative gradient of the Poisson loss $e^F - yF$ is $y - e^F$, which at round 0 is the same −18, −10, −14, 6, 12, 24. The same split wins.

The leaf value is a Newton step on the log scale, $\sum(y - \mu)/\sum\mu$. Left: $-42/84 =$ **−0.5**. Right: $42/84 =$ **+0.5**. After half a step:

$$
28\,e^{-0.25} \approx \mathbf{21.81} \qquad 28\,e^{+0.25} \approx \mathbf{35.95}
$$

Squared error moved both groups by **±7 drills**. Poisson moved them by **×0.78 and ×1.28**. Effects multiply, which is how demand usually behaves.

</Step>
<Step>

**Quantile τ = 0.9** (for safety stock). Start at the 90th percentile of sales, **46**. Under-predicted stores get a pseudo-residual of +0.9, over-predicted ones −0.1. Only store F (52) is above 46, so the first stump splits at **80**, isolating F. The model creeps upward until about 10% of stores sit above it.

</Step>
<Step>

**XGBoost's Newton step with regularization.** XGBoost uses the gradient $g$ *and* the curvature $h$ of the loss. For a leaf, $w^* = -G/(H + \lambda)$. For squared error $h = 1$, so the Keystone's left leaf has $G = 42$, $H = 3$:

| λ | Left leaf $w^*$ | Split gain | Store A after one round |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $-42/3 = -14$ | 588 | 21 |
| 3 | $-42/6 = -7$ | 294 | **24.5** |

And with **γ = 600**, the split's gain (588) doesn't cover the price of a new leaf, so XGBoost **refuses the split** and the tree stays a single leaf. λ shrinks leaves; γ prunes splits.

(For Poisson, $h = \mu$ and $w^* = \sum(y - \mu)/(\sum\mu + \lambda)$: the step you just took in the Poisson row, plus λ.)

</Step>
</Steps>

<Section id="formula">

**Boosting as gradient descent in function space.** Let $F$ be the vector of current predictions, one per training row. Ordinary gradient descent on those predictions would do

$$
F_i \leftarrow F_i - \eta\,\frac{\partial L(y_i, F_i)}{\partial F_i}
$$

But that only moves the training rows; it says nothing about a new store. So gradient boosting fits a small tree $h_m$ to the negative gradients (the pseudo-residuals $r_i = -\partial L/\partial F_i$), then steps along the tree:

$$
F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \eta\, h_m(x)
$$

The tree is a smooth-enough copy of the gradient that can be evaluated anywhere. Each of its leaves then gets the best constant step for that loss (mean, median, quantile or Newton step).

<Depth min={2}>

**The second-order step.** Expand the loss around the current prediction for each row: $L(y_i, F_i + w) \approx L(y_i, F_i) + g_i w + \tfrac12 h_i w^2$, with $g_i = \partial L/\partial F$ and $h_i = \partial^2 L/\partial F^2$. Adding XGBoost's penalty $\tfrac12\lambda w^2$ for a leaf holding rows $I$:

$$
\sum_{i\in I}\left(g_i w + \tfrac12 h_i w^2\right) + \tfrac12\lambda w^2 = G w + \tfrac12 (H + \lambda) w^2
$$

This is a parabola in $w$. Its minimum is at $w^* = -G/(H + \lambda)$ and its value there is $-\tfrac12 G^2/(H + \lambda)$. A split replaces one parabola with two, so

$$
\text{Gain} = \tfrac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma
$$

Every loss reduces to two numbers per row, $g$ and $h$. That's why XGBoost supports any twice-differentiable loss with one tree-building routine.

| Loss | $g$ | $h$ |
|---|---|---|
| Squared $\tfrac12(y - F)^2$ | $F - y$ | 1 |
| Poisson (log link) | $e^F - y$ | $e^F$ |
| Logistic (log loss, logit link) | $p - y$ | $p(1 - p)$ |

`min_child_weight` is a lower bound on $H$ in each child. For squared error that's a minimum row count. For log loss it's subtler: rows the model is already sure about have $p(1 - p) \approx 0$ and barely count.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

**Why shrinkage works.** With a small learning rate, boosting traces something close to an L1-regularized path over the space of all possible trees (Efron, Hastie, Johnstone and Tibshirani's forward-stagewise connection). Early stopping then picks a point on that path, playing the role of the penalty strength.

**Stochastic gradient boosting.** Friedman (2002) found that fitting each tree on a random subsample of rows (typically 50–80%) improves accuracy as well as speed: it decorrelates successive trees, much like bagging.

**AdaBoost is a special case.** AdaBoost is forward-stagewise additive modelling with the exponential loss $e^{-yF}$. Its row weights are exactly that loss's gradients, so "re-weighting the misclassified rows" and "fitting the negative gradient" are two descriptions of one idea.

**Consistency.** With shrinkage, a growing number of rounds and early stopping, boosting is consistent under mild conditions (Zhang and Yu, 2005). Without early stopping it can, and on noisy data does, overfit.

</Depth>

</Section>

<Section id="knobs">

<GBMKnobs />

What each knob does in this playground (60 trees, learning rate 0.3, depth 3 unless changed):

| Knob | What it controls | Effect here |
|---|---|---|
| `max_depth` | How many features each tree can combine (interaction order) | Depth 3 overfits (test 10.3, train 0.7). Depth 1 scores 8.6, because one feature needs no interactions. |
| `reg_lambda` (λ) | Shrinks every leaf: $w^* = -G/(H + \lambda)$ | λ = 20 cuts test error from 10.3 to 8.9 |
| `gamma` (γ) | Minimum gain to keep a split | γ = 100 prunes 427 leaves to 79; test 8.8 |
| `min_child_weight` | Minimum hessian (≈ rows) per leaf | 10 gives a modest gain here (10.1) |
| `subsample` | Share of rows each tree sees | On its own, 0.5 barely helps (10.4); it pays off with lower learning rates and many trees |
| `learning_rate` × `n_estimators` | Step size and step count | See below |

<BoostingOverfit />

The learning rate and the number of trees trade off: half the learning rate needs roughly twice the trees. The rule in practice: fix a small learning rate (0.03–0.1), set `n_estimators` high, and let **early stopping** on a time-based validation set choose when to stop.

</Section>

<Section id="failure">

<FailureLab focus="gbm" start="trend" />

**Where gradient boosting breaks:**

| Situation | What goes wrong | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Trend beyond the training range | Flat forecasts: a sum of trees is still piecewise constant | Detrend the target, add a linear base (`linear_tree` in LightGBM), or model growth separately |
| Outliers with squared loss | Spends rounds chasing one row (test 44 vs 19 for the line) | Huber or absolute loss, or clean the data |
| Tiny data | Drives training error to zero and fits noise | Early stopping, λ, γ, shallow trees, or just a linear model |
| Monotone business rules | Wiggles up in places (12% of the price range here) | `monotone_constraints` |
| Leakage from target encoding | High-cardinality IDs encoded with their own targets look predictive in training | Out-of-fold encoding or CatBoost's ordered statistics (next page) |
| No early stopping | Test error creeps up as rounds accumulate | A time-based validation set |

**Where it wins:** step changes, interactions and most real tabular problems, where it's usually the model to beat.

</Section>

<Section id="build">

From scratch with absolute and Poisson loss, then the libraries. Expected: absolute-error predictions 20 and 33, Poisson 21.81 and 35.95, XGBoost with λ = 3 giving 24.5 and 31.5.

<PyRunner title="Boosting with three losses, three libraries" code={`
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor, HistGradientBoostingRegressor
import xgboost as xgb

x = np.array([20, 30, 40, 55, 70, 90], dtype=float)
y = np.array([10, 18, 14, 34, 40, 52], dtype=float)
X = x.reshape(-1, 1)
lr = 0.5

def best_stump(x, target):
    best = None
    for t in (np.unique(x)[:-1] + np.unique(x)[1:]) / 2:
        l, r = target[x < t], target[x >= t]
        sse = ((l - l.mean())**2).sum() + ((r - r.mean())**2).sum()
        if best is None or sse < best[0]:
            best = (sse, t)
    return best[1]

# Absolute error: start at the median, fit the signs, re-estimate leaves with medians
F = np.full_like(y, np.median(y))
t = best_stump(x, np.sign(y - F))
left = x < t
step = np.where(left, np.median((y - F)[left]), np.median((y - F)[~left]))
print("absolute, from scratch:", F + lr * step)
sk = GradientBoostingRegressor(loss="absolute_error", n_estimators=1, learning_rate=lr, max_depth=1).fit(X, y)
print("absolute, scikit-learn:", sk.predict(X))

# Poisson: raw score is log(rate); leaf = sum(y - mu) / sum(mu)
F = np.full_like(y, np.log(y.mean()))
mu = np.exp(F)
t = best_stump(x, y - mu)
left = x < t
step = np.where(left, (y - mu)[left].sum() / mu[left].sum(), (y - mu)[~left].sum() / mu[~left].sum())
print("poisson, from scratch: ", np.exp(F + lr * step).round(2))
hgb = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="poisson", max_iter=1, learning_rate=lr, max_depth=1, min_samples_leaf=1).fit(X, y)
print("poisson, scikit-learn: ", hgb.predict(X).round(2))

# XGBoost's leaf -G/(H + lambda): lambda = 0 reproduces the Keystone, lambda = 3 halves the step
for lam in [0, 3]:
    m = xgb.XGBRegressor(n_estimators=1, max_depth=1, learning_rate=lr, reg_lambda=lam,
                         base_score=28, tree_method="exact", min_child_weight=0).fit(X, y)
    print(f"xgboost, lambda={lam}:   ", m.predict(X))
`} />

<Section id="production">

- **Choose the objective from the noise and the decision.** `tweedie` or `poisson` for unit sales, `quantile` for service levels, `binary` for conversion, `huber` when a few bulk orders shouldn't steer the model. (See [MLE and Losses](/learn/bayes/mle-peak).)
- **Validate on time, not at random.** Hold out the latest weeks; use the validation loss for early stopping. Random splits leak the future through lag features and seasonality.
- **Feature engineering still matters.** Lags, rolling means, price relative to the category, days to Diwali. Trees can't compute "price ÷ last month's price" on their own unless you give it to them.
- **Global models.** One LightGBM over all SKU × store series, with store and SKU attributes as features, usually beats thousands of per-series models: it shares information about promotions and seasonality across items.
- **Monitoring.** Track SHAP-based feature attributions alongside accuracy. A sudden swing in the importance of "promo flag" is often a data pipeline change.
- **Serving.** A few hundred trees evaluate in microseconds per row. Export with Treelite or ONNX for low-latency services, or score in batch with Spark.

</Section>

<Exercise n={1} level="easy" title="One more round, by hand">

After round 1 with absolute loss the predictions are 20, 20, 20, 33, 33, 33. What are the pseudo-residuals for round 2?

<Solution>

Residuals: $10 - 20 = -10$, $-2$, $-6$, $1$, $7$, $19$. Signs: **−1, −1, −1, +1, +1, +1**. Same pattern as round 1, so the next stump likely splits at 47.5 again; its leaves are the median residuals, −6 and +7.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} level="easy" title="Gradient and hessian for log loss">

A conversion model predicts $p = 0.8$ for a shopper who didn't buy ($y = 0$). What are $g$ and $h$?

<Solution>

$g = p - y =$ **0.8**, $h = p(1 - p) =$ **0.16**. The next tree is pushed to lower this shopper's score. A confident correct prediction ($p = 0.99$, $y = 1$) has $h = 0.0099$: it hardly counts toward `min_child_weight`.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} level="medium" title="λ by hand">

A leaf holds three rows with residuals 4, 6 and 8 (squared loss). What's its XGBoost value with λ = 0, 3 and 9?

<Solution>

$G = -(4 + 6 + 8) = -18$, $H = 3$. $w^* = 18/(3 + \lambda)$: **6**, **3** and **1.5**. λ acts like λ extra rows with residual 0 pulling the leaf toward zero.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} level="medium" title="When γ blocks a split">

A split has $G_L = -30$, $H_L = 5$, $G_R = 20$, $H_R = 5$, λ = 0. What's the gain before γ, and what's the largest γ that still allows it?

<Solution>

$\tfrac12[900/5 + 400/5 - 100/10] = \tfrac12[180 + 80 - 10] =$ **125**. Any γ below 125 keeps the split; at γ ≥ 125 it's refused.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} level="hard" title="Poisson's multiplicative step">

Show that one Poisson boosting step with leaf value $w$ and learning rate η multiplies every prediction in that leaf by the same factor. Why is that a good match for retail demand?

<Solution>

The raw score is $\log\mu$, and the step adds $\eta w$ to it, so $\mu_{\text{new}} = e^{F + \eta w} = \mu\,e^{\eta w}$: every store in the leaf is scaled by $e^{\eta w}$. Retail effects (a promotion, a festival week) tend to lift demand by a percentage, not by a fixed number of units: a big store gains more units than a small one. Additive steps on squared error would give both the same number of units.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} level="hard" title="Why not just fit the residuals for every loss?">

Someone proposes: "For quantile loss, just fit trees to the plain residuals $y - F$ like always." What goes wrong?

<Solution>

Fitting $y - F$ minimizes **squared** error no matter which loss you claim to use, so the model converges to the conditional **mean**, not the 90th percentile. The pseudo-residual (+0.9 / −0.1) is what encodes "being under is nine times worse than being over". And the leaf values must be quantiles of the residuals, not means, for the same reason.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Section id="interview">

<QA q="What exactly does each tree fit in gradient boosting, for a general loss?">

The negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current prediction, evaluated at each training row (pseudo-residuals). After the tree's structure is chosen, each leaf's value is set to the best constant step for that loss within the leaf: the mean residual for squared error, the median for absolute error, a quantile for pinball loss, or the Newton step −G/(H + λ) in XGBoost.

</QA>

<QA q="What's the difference between XGBoost's objective and classic GBM?">

Classic GBM uses only the gradient to build the tree and then a line search per leaf. XGBoost uses a second-order Taylor expansion (gradient and hessian) for both the leaf values, w* = −G/(H + λ), and the split gain, and adds explicit regularization: λ (L2 on leaf values), α (L1) and γ (a cost per leaf). That makes the split criterion and the leaf values consistent with the regularized objective.

</QA>

<QA q="Your boosted demand model predicts flat sales for next Diwali even though sales grow 15% a year. Why, and how do you fix it?">

Trees can't extrapolate: next Diwali's feature values lie beyond the training range, so every tree returns its edge leaf. Fix it by modelling growth outside the trees: predict a ratio to a trend or last year's level, difference the target, add a linear base learner (`linear_tree`), or blend with a model that can extrapolate.

</QA>

<QA q="How would you tune a GBM under time pressure?">

Fix a small learning rate (0.05), use early stopping on a time-based validation set to pick the number of trees, then tune tree complexity (num_leaves or max_depth, min_child_samples), then regularization (λ, γ, min_child_weight) and subsampling (rows and columns). Tune the objective before any of that: the right loss usually matters more than the last hyperparameter.

</QA>

<Trap>

**"Boosting can't overfit, because each tree is weak."** It can and does: training error keeps falling as rounds are added, and test error turns back up. Weak learners make each step small, not the sum of hundreds of steps. That's why early stopping is part of the algorithm, not an optional extra.

</Trap>
