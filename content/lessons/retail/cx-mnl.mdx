<Section id="floor">

About **800** shoppers a week stop at Bolt & Beam's cordless drill bay. Three drills are on the shelf: the **Kestrel 20V** at ₹4,500, the **own-label 18V** at ₹3,000 and a **budget 12V** at ₹2,000. Some shoppers leave without a drill.

Two decisions are on the table. Kestrel's supplier has missed a delivery, so Kestrel will be out of stock for a month: how many of its buyers switch, and to what? And the supplier wants to add a **red Kestrel**, identical except for color: how much will it really add? A choice model answers both. It also shows where its one big assumption breaks.

<KeyIdea>

**Each shopper picks the option with the highest utility: a value everyone shares plus a random personal taste.** If the random part follows a Gumbel distribution, the choice shares are exactly a **softmax**: $s_j = e^{V_j} / \sum_k e^{V_k}$. The plain logit has one strong assumption, **IIA** (independence of irrelevant alternatives): when an option disappears, its buyers spread over the rest *in proportion to their shares*. A **nested logit** relaxes it by grouping close substitutes.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section id="hand">

Utilities (value net of price) for a typical shopper: Kestrel **1.39**, own label **0.69**, budget **0**, and leaving with no drill **0**. Those are $\ln 4$, $\ln 2$, $\ln 1$ and $\ln 1$, chosen to keep the arithmetic easy.

<Steps>
<Step>

**Exponentiate:** $e^{1.39} = 4$, $e^{0.69} = 2$, $e^0 = 1$, $e^0 = 1$. Sum: **8**.

</Step>
<Step>

**Divide by the sum:** Kestrel **50%**, own label **25%**, budget **12.5%**, no drill **12.5%**. Of 800 shoppers: 400, 200, 100 and 100.

</Step>
<Step>

**Kestrel out of stock.** Drop its 4: the sum is $2 + 1 + 1 = 4$. Own label **50%**, budget **25%**, no drill **25%**: 400, 200, 200. Kestrel's 400 buyers split 200 / 100 / 100, in proportion to the remaining shares, so a quarter of them go home without a drill. That is IIA.

</Step>
<Step>

**A price cut.** The own label drops from ₹3,000 to ₹2,500. Each ₹1,000 costs 0.8 of utility, so its utility rises by 0.4 and $e^{V}$ goes from 2 to $2e^{0.4} = 2.98$. Sum 8.98: Kestrel **44.5%**, own label **33.2%**, budget **11.1%**, no drill **11.1%**. Every other option lost the same 11% of its share.

</Step>
<Step>

**Add a red Kestrel**, identical to the blue one: $4 + 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 12$. The two Kestrels together get **66.7%**, and the own label falls to **16.7%**: a third of its buyers lost to a change of color. That can't be right. The red Kestrel should mostly split the blue one's buyers.

</Step>
</Steps>

</Section>

<Section id="simulate">

Where does the softmax come from? Simulate it. Give each shopper a random taste for each option (utility = the hand value + noise), and let them pick the biggest. Count the choices:

<RandomUtility />

| Shoppers | Gumbel noise: biggest gap to softmax | Normal noise: biggest gap |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 5.0 points | 2.0 points |
| 1,000 | 1.2 | 2.2 |
| 10,000 | 0.8 | 0.7 |
| 100,000 | 0.2 | 1.0 |

- **With Gumbel noise the counts converge to the softmax** (gap 0.2 points at 100,000 shoppers). That's not an approximation: it's a theorem.
- **With normal noise of the same spread** (a *probit* model) the shares settle near 51.0%, 25.5%, 11.8% and 11.7%, not exactly 50 / 25 / 12.5 / 12.5. Close, but no formula. Gumbel noise is chosen because it makes the shares come out in closed form.

</Section>

<Section id="formula">

Shopper $n$ gets utility $U_{nj} = V_j + e_{nj}$ from option $j$ and chooses the largest. With the $e$'s independent and Gumbel distributed, $F(e) = \exp(-e^{-e})$:

$$
P(j) = P\big(V_j + e_j > V_k + e_k \text{ for all } k \ne j\big) = \frac{e^{V_j}}{\sum_k e^{V_k}}
$$

Only differences in utility matter: adding a constant to every $V$ cancels. Utilities are built from attributes and price, $V_j = \alpha_j - \beta p_j$, with $\beta = 0.8$ per ₹1,000 here.

**IIA in one line.** The ratio of two shares, $s_j / s_k = e^{V_j - V_k}$, doesn't depend on any other option. Remove or add an option and every ratio stays the same, which is what forced the proportional split.

**Elasticities from the logit.** Differentiating the softmax:

$$
\varepsilon_{jj} = -\beta p_j (1 - s_j), \qquad \varepsilon_{kj} = \beta p_j s_j \quad (k \ne j)
$$

Kestrel's own elasticity: $-0.8 \times 4.5 \times 0.5 = -1.8$. And *every* other option responds to Kestrel's price with the same $+1.8$; to the own label's price, $0.8 \times 3 \times 0.25 = 0.6$; to the budget drill's, 0.2. A whole column of the cross-elasticity matrix is one number: IIA again.

<Depth min={2}>

**Fitting a logit is minimizing cross-entropy.** The log-likelihood of observed choices is $\sum_n \ln s_{n,\text{chosen}}$: the negative of the cross-entropy between the observed choices and the predicted shares. Its gradient for $\beta$ is (observed − predicted) price, the same shape as logistic regression, which is the two-option logit.

**Backing out β from shares.** Against the "no drill" option, $\ln(s_j / s_0) = V_j$. At ₹3,000 the own label's log-odds are $\ln(0.25/0.125) = 0.69$; at ₹2,500, $\ln(0.332/0.111) = 1.09$. A ₹500 cut raised them by 0.4, so $\beta = 0.8$ per ₹1,000. The same inversion (Berry's) is how market-share data are turned into utilities.

**Nested logit.** Group options into nests with a parameter $\lambda \in (0, 1]$. Inside a nest, shares are a softmax of $V_j/\lambda$. Each nest competes with weight $e^{\lambda I}$, where $I = \ln \sum_{j \in \text{nest}} e^{V_j/\lambda}$ is the **inclusive value**. For the two Kestrels at λ = 0.5: $I = \ln(16 + 16) = \ln 32$, weight $32^{0.5} = 5.66$, against $2 + 1 + 1$ for the rest: the Kestrels get $5.66/9.66 = 58.6\%$.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

**The derivation.** Condition on $e_j$: option $j$ wins if every $e_k \lt V_j - V_k + e_j$. So

$$
P(j) = \int f(e_j) \prod_{k \ne j} F(V_j - V_k + e_j)\, de_j
$$

With $F(x) = \exp(-e^{-x})$ the product is $\exp\!\big(-e^{-e_j} \sum_{k \ne j} e^{V_k - V_j}\big)$, and the integral collapses to $e^{V_j} / \sum_k e^{V_k}$.

**The max of Gumbels is Gumbel.** The best utility on offer, $\max_j U_j$, is itself Gumbel with location $\ln \sum_j e^{V_j}$. So the **log-sum-exp is the expected best utility** (up to Euler's constant): the consumer surplus of a line-up, in utility units. Divide by β to get rupees. Dropping Kestrel cuts it from $\ln 8$ to $\ln 4$: $0.69/0.8 = $ ₹866 per shopper. That's the inclusive value again, and why the softmax shows up wherever a "soft max" is needed: contrastive learning and attention.

**Other ways out of IIA.** Mixed logit lets β and the α's vary across shoppers (a distribution over tastes), so price-sensitive shoppers substitute toward cheap drills and brand-loyal ones toward Kestrel. It breaks IIA at the cost of simulation in fitting.

</Depth>

</Section>

<Section id="build">

The hand shares, then 100,000 simulated shoppers with Gumbel tastes, then a logit fitted by maximum likelihood to 20,000 simulated choices at varying own-label prices. Expected: shares 0.500, 0.250, 0.125, 0.125; the simulation within a fraction of a point; a fitted β close to 0.8.

<PyRunner title="Softmax from random utility" code={`
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

V = np.log([4, 2, 1, 1])  # Kestrel, own label, budget, no drill
shares = np.exp(V) / np.exp(V).sum()
print("softmax shares:", ", ".join(f"{s:.3f}" for s in shares))

rng = np.random.default_rng(31)
taste = rng.gumbel(size=(100_000, 4))
picks = np.argmax(V + taste, axis=1)
print("100,000 shoppers:", ", ".join(f"{s:.3f}" for s in np.bincount(picks, minlength=4) / len(picks)))

# Fit beta: 20,000 shoppers, own-label price varies from week to week
n = 20_000
prices = np.array([4.5, 3.0, 2.0])               # in thousands of rupees
alpha = np.log([4, 2, 1]) + 0.8 * prices
own = rng.choice([2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0], size=n)
P = np.tile(prices, (n, 1)); P[:, 1] = own
U = np.column_stack([alpha - 0.8 * P, np.zeros(n)]) + rng.gumbel(size=(n, 4))
chosen = np.argmax(U, axis=1)

def nll(theta):
    a, b = theta[:3], theta[3]
    v = np.column_stack([a - b * P, np.zeros(n)])
    v = v - v.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    logp = v - np.log(np.exp(v).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return -logp[np.arange(n), chosen].sum()

fit = minimize(nll, np.zeros(4))
print(f"fitted beta {fit.x[3]:.2f} per 1,000 rupees (truth 0.80)")
`} />

<Section id="production">

- **Assortment and delisting.** A choice model predicts where a delisted product's buyers go (demand transfer) and how much of its volume the bay keeps. That's the core of assortment optimization; stockouts, as natural experiments, test whether the model's transfer is right.
- **The no-purchase option needs traffic.** Transaction data shows only buyers. To learn the outside share you need footfall or bay-visit counts (door counters, app sessions), or the "no drill" share has to be assumed.
- **Nests come from the customer decision tree.** Do shoppers choose power first (20V vs 18V) or brand first? The nesting order is a modeling choice, testable by which one fits better, and it decides who cannibalizes whom.
- **Aggregate data and endogenous prices.** With only store-week shares, use the log-share inversion with prices instrumented, because prices respond to demand ([BLP-style models](/learn/retail/cx-endogeneity)).
- **Where it fails.** The plain logit makes every cross-elasticity in a column equal, so it over-predicts switching to dissimilar products and under-predicts it to close substitutes. Check its predicted transfers against a real stockout before using it to delist.

</Section>

<Section id="interview">

<QA q="Derive the softmax choice probabilities from random utility.">

Utility = deterministic value + i.i.d. Gumbel noise; the shopper picks the max. Conditioning on one noise term and integrating the Gumbel CDFs of the others gives $e^{V_j}/\sum_k e^{V_k}$. Only utility differences matter, and the noise scale sets how sharp the shares are.

</QA>

<QA q="What is IIA and why does it matter for retail?">

The ratio of any two shares doesn't depend on other options. So when a product is delisted, its buyers spread proportionally to the others' shares, and adding a near-duplicate takes share from everyone equally. Real shoppers switch to close substitutes, so a plain logit misjudges cannibalization and delisting. Nested or mixed logit fix this.

</QA>

<QA q="How would you predict demand transfer when an item is delisted?">

Fit a choice model with product attributes and nests from the customer decision tree, then remove the item and recompute shares; the change is the transfer, including the share that walks away. Validate it on past stockouts, where the item was briefly unavailable, before acting.

</QA>

<QA q="How do you estimate a logit model?">

Maximum likelihood: the log-likelihood is the sum of log predicted shares of the chosen options, i.e. minus the cross-entropy. It's convex in the linear utility parameters. With aggregate shares, invert to $\ln(s_j/s_0)$ and regress on attributes and price, instrumenting price.

</QA>

<Trap>

**"The logit tells you where a delisted product's buyers will go."** Only under IIA, which sends them everywhere in proportion to share: a quarter of Kestrel's buyers "walk away" because no-drill has a quarter of the remaining share. Check the transfer against a real stockout, or use a nested model.

</Trap>
