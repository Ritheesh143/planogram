<Section id="floor">

The Kharadi store sold **0, 2 and 1** cordless drills on three slow weekdays. The replenishment system needs one number: **λ, the average drills sold per day**. It drives the reorder point and the safety stock.

A colleague says: *"λ = 1 has a much higher likelihood than λ = 5."*

What does that sentence actually claim? **Likelihood** is one of the most important words in data science:
- Maximum likelihood estimation uses it.
- Almost every loss function you've trained with (MSE, cross-entropy, LightGBM's `poisson` and `tweedie` objectives) is built from it.
- Bayes' theorem multiplies by it.

This lesson pins it down with a dial.

<KeyIdea>

**Probability:** fix the parameter, then ask how probable different *data* are. *"If λ = 1, how often would a day sell 2 drills?"*

**Likelihood:** fix the data you actually saw, then ask how well different *parameters* explain it. *"Given that we saw 0, 2, 1, how well does λ = 1 explain that, compared with λ = 5?"*

It's the same formula read in two directions.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section id="hand">

The **Poisson model** says that if the store averages λ drills per day, the chance of selling exactly $k$ in a day is

$$
P(k \mid \lambda) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}
$$

(Take this formula on trust for now. *Where Poisson Comes From* derives it from customers arriving at the counter.)

<Steps>
<Step>

**A probability question, with λ fixed at 1.** Compute each day separately:

| Day | Sold $k$ | $P(k \mid \lambda = 1)$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | $e^{-1} = 0.368$ |
| 2 | 2 | $\tfrac{1}{2} e^{-1} = 0.184$ |
| 3 | 1 | $e^{-1} = 0.368$ |

</Step>
<Step>

**The whole 3-day pattern.** Days are independent, so multiply:

$$
0.368 \times 0.184 \times 0.368 = 0.0249
$$

If λ were 1, you'd see exactly this 0, 2, 1 sequence about **2.5% of the time**.

</Step>
<Step>

**Same data, now try λ = 5.**

$P(0) = e^{-5} = 0.0067$, $P(2) = 12.5\, e^{-5} = 0.0842$, $P(1) = 5\, e^{-5} = 0.0337$.

Product: $0.0067 \times 0.0842 \times 0.0337 = 0.000019$.

</Step>
<Step>

**Compare.** $0.0249 / 0.000019 \approx 1{,}300$.

The data are about **1,300 times more probable under λ = 1 than under λ = 5**. That, and only that, is what *"λ = 1 has a higher likelihood"* means. It says nothing about the probability that λ = 1 is *true*. That would need a prior, which is Bayes.

</Step>
<Step>

**Turn the dial in steps.** Do the same product for more values of λ:

| λ | 0.5 | 1.0 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 5.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Likelihood $L(\lambda)$ | 0.0139 | **0.0249** | 0.0188 | 0.0099 | 0.000019 |

It rises, peaks at **λ = 1**, then falls. Plot these points and you have the **likelihood curve**. Its peak is the **maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)**. Notice that 1 = (0 + 2 + 1) / 3, the plain average.

</Step>
</Steps>

</Section>

<Section id="formula">

**Step 1: the likelihood is a product.** For data $k_1, \dots, k_n$:

$$
L(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\lambda^{k_i} e^{-\lambda}}{k_i!}
$$

**Step 2: take logs to turn the product into a sum.** Logs turn × into +, and the peak stays in the same place:

$$
\ell(\lambda) = \log L(\lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big( k_i \log\lambda - \lambda - \log k_i! \big) = \Big(\sum_i k_i\Big)\log\lambda - n\lambda - \text{const}
$$

**Step 3: find the peak by setting the derivative to zero.**

$$
\frac{d\ell}{d\lambda} = \frac{\sum_i k_i}{\lambda} - n = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat\lambda = \frac{\sum_i k_i}{n} = \bar k
$$

The second derivative is $-\sum k_i / \lambda^2 \lt 0$, so this is a maximum. **The Poisson MLE is the average.** For 0, 2, 1 that's 1.

<Depth min={2} label="Loss functions are negative log-likelihoods">

## Your loss functions, unmasked

Training a model means *minimizing a loss*. Most standard losses are just **minus a log-likelihood**, so minimizing them is maximum likelihood.

**Gaussian noise gives MSE.** If $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$, then

$$
-\log L = \sum_i \frac{(y_i - f(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const}
$$

Minimizing this over $f$ is minimizing squared error. **Choosing MSE is quietly assuming Gaussian, constant-variance noise.**

**Bernoulli outcomes give cross-entropy.** If a visitor buys with probability $p$:

$$
-\log L = -\sum_i \big[ y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i) \big]
$$

That's **log loss**, the binary cross-entropy every classifier and every LLM is trained on.

**Poisson counts give Poisson deviance.** That's LightGBM's `objective="poisson"`, built for counts like units sold. A SKU selling 200 a day and one selling 2 a day get noise that scales with their level, instead of MSE's single fixed variance.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3} label="Curvature is certainty">

## How sure is the MLE?

The sharpness of the log-likelihood's peak measures how much the data pin λ down. The curvature at the peak is the **Fisher information**:

$$
I(\hat\lambda) = -\ell''(\hat\lambda) = \frac{\sum k_i}{\hat\lambda^2} = \frac{n}{\hat\lambda}
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
SE(\hat\lambda) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{I}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat\lambda}{n}}
$$

With 3 days, $SE = \sqrt{1/3} = 0.58$, which is very uncertain. With 30 days at the same average, $SE = 0.18$. The curve gets about √10 times narrower. The Build It code shows this.

The values of λ whose log-likelihood is within 1.92 of the peak form an approximate 95% **likelihood interval**. The 1.92 is half of 3.84, the 95% point of a χ² distribution with one degree of freedom.

</Depth>

**Bridge to Bayes:** the Bayesian aisle multiplies this curve by a prior and rescales the result so its area is 1. That's the posterior.

$$
\text{posterior}(\lambda) \propto L(\lambda) \times \text{prior}(\lambda)
$$

</Section>

<Exercise n={3} level="medium" title="Likelihood ratio">

Two days of sales: 3 and 5. How many times more likely is λ = 4 than λ = 2?

<Solution>

$L(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{3+5} e^{-2\lambda} = \lambda^8 e^{-2\lambda}$ (the $k!$ terms cancel in a ratio).

$$
\frac{L(4)}{L(2)} = \Big(\frac{4}{2}\Big)^8 e^{-2(4-2)} = 256 \times e^{-4} = 256 \times 0.0183 \approx 4.7
$$

So λ = 4 is about **4.7×** more likely. The MLE is (3 + 5) / 2 = 4.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} level="medium" title="Which sentence is right?">

Data 0, 2, 1. Which statements are correct?
(a) "λ = 1 is the most probable value of λ."
(b) "λ = 1 makes the observed sales more probable than any other λ does."
(c) "The likelihood curve shows the probability of each λ."

<Solution>

Only **(b)**. Statement (a) needs a prior; without one, there's no probability *of* λ. Statement (c) is wrong because the curve doesn't even integrate to 1.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} level="hard" title="Cross-entropy from scratch">

Out of $n$ visitors to the paint aisle, $k$ buy. Write the Bernoulli log-likelihood for the purchase probability $p$, find the MLE, and show that the negative log-likelihood per visitor is the binary cross-entropy.

<Solution>

$\ell(p) = k \log p + (n-k)\log(1-p)$. The derivative is $k/p - (n-k)/(1-p) = 0$, which gives $\hat p = k/n$.

Per visitor $i$ with outcome $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$:

$$
-\tfrac{1}{n}\ell = -\tfrac1n \sum_i \big[y_i\log p + (1-y_i)\log(1-p)\big]
$$

That's exactly binary cross-entropy. A classifier makes $p$ depend on features, $p_i = \sigma(w^\top x_i)$, but the loss is the same likelihood.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={6} level="hard" title="Stockouts censor demand">

On day 2 the store had only **2** drills on the shelf and sold out. True demand was *at least* 2. Write that day's likelihood term. Treating the day as "demand = 2" pushes $\hat\lambda$ in which direction?

<Solution>

The day contributes $P(D \ge 2 \mid \lambda) = 1 - e^{-\lambda}(1 + \lambda)$ instead of $P(D = 2 \mid \lambda)$. That term **keeps increasing** with λ (more demand makes a sell-out likelier), so it pulls the MLE **up**. Treating sales as demand biases λ **down**, and the store under-orders, which causes more stockouts. This is the core of the *Demand ≠ Sales* lesson in the forecasting signature.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Section id="build">

Find the MLE three ways (grid, optimizer, formula). Then compare how narrow the plausible range gets with 3 days vs 30 days of data.

<PyRunner title="MLE three ways" code={`
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import poisson
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

sales = np.array([0, 2, 1])

grid = np.linspace(0.05, 6, 600)
lik = np.array([poisson.pmf(sales, lam).prod() for lam in grid])
loglik = np.array([poisson.logpmf(sales, lam).sum() for lam in grid])
print("grid MLE from likelihood:     ", round(grid[lik.argmax()], 3))
print("grid MLE from log-likelihood: ", round(grid[loglik.argmax()], 3))

neg_ll = lambda lam: -poisson.logpmf(sales, lam).sum()
res = minimize_scalar(neg_ll, bounds=(1e-6, 20), method="bounded")
print("optimizer MLE:                ", round(res.x, 4))
print("sample mean:                  ", sales.mean())

# More data, same average -> a narrower likelihood (more certainty)
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
month = rng.poisson(1.0, size=30)
for name, data in [("3 days", sales), ("30 days", month)]:
    ll = np.array([poisson.logpmf(data, lam).sum() for lam in grid])
    inside = grid[ll > ll.max() - 1.92]   # approx. 95% likelihood interval
    print(f"{name:>7}: MLE {data.mean():.2f}, plausible lambda from {inside.min():.2f} to {inside.max():.2f}")
`} />

<Section id="production">

**Where likelihood shows up in production retail systems:**

- **Choosing a loss means choosing a noise model.** For unit sales, MSE treats an error of 5 the same on a 2-a-day SKU and a 500-a-day SKU. Poisson and Tweedie objectives (LightGBM, XGBoost) model counts, many zeros and level-dependent noise. Many M5 forecasting winners used Tweedie for exactly this reason.
- **Censored likelihoods for stockouts.** Demand-forecasting systems that train on raw sales learn the shelf capacity, not the demand. Flag stockout days and use $P(D \ge \text{sales})$ for them, or drop or impute them.
- **Numerical stability.** Never multiply thousands of probabilities, because they underflow to 0. Always sum log-probabilities, and use log-sum-exp when you need to add probabilities in log space.
- **Model comparison.** The held-out log-likelihood ("log score") is the standard metric for probabilistic forecasts. AIC and BIC are log-likelihoods penalized for the number of parameters.

</Section>

<Section id="interview">

<QA q="What's the difference between probability and likelihood?">

Probability fixes the parameters and describes how likely different outcomes are, and it sums to 1 over outcomes. Likelihood fixes the observed data and treats the same formula as a function of the parameters. It measures how well each parameter value explains the data and does not sum to 1. Give the example: P(sales = 2 | λ = 1) vs L(λ | sales = 0, 2, 1).

</QA>

<QA q="Derive the MLE of a Poisson rate.">

Write $\ell(\lambda) = \sum k_i \log\lambda - n\lambda - \text{const}$, differentiate to get $\sum k_i/\lambda - n$, set it to 0 to get $\hat\lambda = \bar k$, and check that the second derivative is negative. Then add that the SE is $\sqrt{\hat\lambda / n}$.

</QA>

<QA q="Why is MSE the default regression loss, and when is it the wrong choice?">

It's the negative log-likelihood under Gaussian noise with constant variance. It's wrong when the noise isn't like that. For counts with many zeros, use Poisson or Tweedie. For heavy-tailed errors or outliers, use Huber or absolute error. For a target spanning orders of magnitude, use a log transform or a multiplicative model. When you need quantiles (service levels), use pinball loss.

</QA>

<QA q="Why maximize the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood?">

The log is increasing, so it has the same maximizer. It turns products into sums, which are easier to differentiate and numerically stable (no underflow). And for exponential-family models it often makes the problem concave.

</QA>

<Trap>

**"The MLE is the most probable parameter value."** Without a prior, there's no probability *of* a parameter. The MLE is the parameter under which **the data** are most probable. If you add a prior and take the peak of the posterior, you get the MAP estimate, which is a different thing.

</Trap>
